## Sentence Punctuation

### Common Cues

#### Words That Usually Take a Break Before Them

1. **Sentence-opening particles** such as **夫, 盖, 至若, 若夫** usually allow a break before them
1. **Interrogative pronouns** such as **何, 孰, 曷, 安, 奚** usually allow a break before them
1. **Connective words** such as **虽, 故, 况, 苟, 岂, 然则, 于是** usually allow a break before them
1. **Time words** such as **俄而, 顷之, 未几, 既而** usually allow a break before them

#### Words That Usually Take a Break After Them

**Sentence-final modal particles** such as **也, 矣, 耳, 哉, 耶, 与 (欤), 焉** usually take a break after them

#### Places Where a Break Is Never Allowed on Either Side

When `之` means "of" (i.e. functions as an attributive-head marker), then **a break is never allowed on either side of it**.

▲ Prepositional phrases formed with `于` / `以` generally must stay tight against the preceding element, functioning as a postposed adverbial.  
In the case of postposed adverbials, `于` / `以` generally **cannot take a break on either side**; this also applies when `于` is omitted.

A postposed adverbial generally does not connect to any further elements, so a break generally does occur **after the adverbial**.

#### Words with Different Meanings Then and Now

When a punctuation question involves different possible breaks tied to a word that has changed meaning over time, **discard the modern usage and follow the ancient usage**.

> 晚而改节 / 敦儒学 / 习《尚书》/ 读律令 / 略举大义

### The Parallel/Symmetric Structure Method

* Parallel or similar sentence patterns  
* May appear within the clause to be punctuated, or in the surrounding **context**

> 而口不讼直 / 志不苟免 / 宣帝嘉之 / 赦而不诛  
> 先菽秫 / 次黍麦 / 次稻 / 俟秋成偿官  

### The Subject Method

When **a contradiction appears / the meaning becomes unclear**, consider ruling that reading out.

### Making the Call

When, within a single clause, some readings support a break and others don't, follow these principles:

1. Must not create an obvious grammatical error, ambiguity, or confusion about which object goes with which relation
1. Try to keep the sentence coherent and fluent
1. Must not violate any other principle

## Classical Cultural Knowledge

1. Basic common knowledge
1. `Hardcore` knowledge
1. Knowledge that can be checked by substituting it back into the original text

Among real exam questions, category 1 / 3 accounts for the majority, category 2 for very few

During regular practice, pay attention to the cultural knowledge you encounter in exercises; **there's no need to deliberately memorize** a checklist

### Checking Against the Original Text

Substitute the content of the relevant answer choice back into the original text, and check the **subject(s)** and **relationship(s)** against the original, or compare **similar structures / related content** in the surrounding text.  
If you can find a contradiction or an obvious mismatch, then the answer choice must have a problem.  

### `Original meaning / extended meaning`, `general sense / specific sense`: **you don't need to cover every angle**

Many words have both an original meaning and an extended meaning, both a general sense and a sense specific to the text; it isn't necessary for every possible meaning to match the text perfectly for an answer to be correct. It's enough that **the actual meaning expressed by the answer choice matches the original text**.

## Comprehension/Judgment Multiple Choice Questions

> No need to fully understand the passage; focus on **how to verify the information**  
> The easiest version of the expository-text reading question type  

### Quick Localization

1. The original text and the answer choices generally follow a strict **chronological order**
1. Changes in **people's names, place names, and official titles** are often important clues
1. **Attitudes tied to characters tend to be clear-cut** (stereotyped): historical texts tend to express a clear emotional stance toward the protagonist, and the protagonist's emotional stance toward related events also tends to be clear
1. The **punctuation** of classical Chinese passages on the college entrance exam is very carefully done and won't be broken arbitrarily, so it can also serve as a reference

### Obvious Contradictions

Basically only the **most obvious and central contradiction** is tested  
Usually you only need to check the **main** `subject` and `relationship`  
It rarely even requires inference or logical transformation  
It rarely involves `cross-referencing` text from different locations  

### Catch the Contradiction, `See It Clearly`, Don't Overthink

* Direct → indirect
* Whole → part
* Certain → second-guessing

## The Seven-Step Method for Translating Classical Chinese: `Break apart → Find → Split → Order → List → Choose → Connect`

> The core three steps: `Split → List → Choose`

### Step One: `Break Apart`

Break the sentence down into `each individual word`

### Step Two: `Find`

> Find the known conditions

1. Content that `doesn't need translating`
1. Content that is `already certain`
1. Related information from the surrounding text

### Step Three: `Split`

Separate out the `known` and `unknown` content

### Step Four: `Order`

1. Rank the unknown content by `difficulty`
1. Prioritize the `easier` parts first

### Step Five: `List`

> For the unknown words, list out as many known meanings as possible  
> Consider the following methods  

1. List `dictionary senses`
1. Try `forming compound words`
1. Try `substituting in` a familiar phrase

### Step Six: `Choose`

> Using the known conditions, choose the most fitting meaning for the unknown word

1. Draw on the known conditions from two main angles: `grammar (part of speech)` / `semantics`
1. If unsure, check for `flexible part-of-speech usage (sentence-pattern shifts)` / the possibility of conveying the sense loosely

> `List` / `Choose` may need to be repeated several times

### Step Seven: `Connect`

Make sure it is **rendered word by word, and reads smoothly**

> Translate literally wherever possible; where a literal translation isn't possible, `convey the sense`, making sure every word is accounted for, though `function words` don't necessarily need to be forced into the translation  
> The standard for `conveying the sense`: **correct meaning + correct grammar**, **no embellishment added**

1. No embellishment: during translation, do not arbitrarily add **content words** or function words expressing **strict relationships**
1. No selective blindness: during translation, do not selectively ignore any words in the sentence
