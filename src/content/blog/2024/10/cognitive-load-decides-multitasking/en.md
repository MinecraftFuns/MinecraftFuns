Multitasking names the ability to run more than one task at a time, and the
literature does not agree that anyone has it. One reading of the evidence is
true simultaneous attention. The other is rapid switching between tasks that
never really overlap, a workaround for limited cognitive resources. Both
describe real behavior, and the variable that decides which one you get is
the cognitive load of the task.

## Tracking several things at once

Supporters of simultaneous attention point at people doing it under
measurement. In the multi-object tracking task, observers follow several
moving objects at the same time and keep hold of about four or five of them
([Cowan](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922);
[Intriligator and Cavanagh](https://doi.org/10.1006/cogp.2001.0755)).
Whatever the exact number, the brain is managing several streams of
information rather than one.

## The bottleneck

[Pashler](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.2.220) reads the dual-task
experiments the other way. Even simple tasks compete for cognitive
resources, and two speeded tasks presented close together interfere
noticeably. He puts the interference in a central bottleneck: response
selection and memory retrieval take longer when two tasks demand them
together, which delays the second task.

The psychological refractory period is that delay, measured. Present two
stimuli close together in time and the response to the second is reliably
slower. On this account, what looks like doing two things is finishing one
and then starting the other.

## Where the disagreement sits

The dispute is about cognitive capacity and about what attention is. One
side holds that multitasking fits inside the brain's limits and works. The
other points at dual-task studies where performance drops sharply under
load, and reads that drop as switching.

Task complexity is the second axis, and both sides move along it. Simple
tasks leave room for real overlap. Complex ones raise response latency,
which is what you would expect from a brain that has fallen back to taking
turns.

## My position

Both mechanisms are real, and the mind runs the cheaper one until it cannot.

Simultaneous attention is what the architecture does by default, and the
evidence is sitting in every waking moment. The sensory organs relay
continuously, and the brain builds one representation of the outside world
out of that influx. Managing and integrating many sensations at once is the
ordinary condition.

Rapid switching is what the same system falls back on when the load stops
fitting: during a complex task, or when several tasks want the same central
bottleneck. Attention then narrows to one task or a few sources, and moves
between them.

## Why both accounts survive

The sensory argument does most of the work. Each modality delivers
information continuously and the brain consolidates all of it into one
coherent understanding of the environment, which puts multitasking in our
cognitive architecture rather than in a skill some people have.

Pashler's bottleneck does not contradict that. It says interference appears
when two tasks need similar cognitive resources, and that the brain has to
prioritize to keep from swamping its capacity. It says nothing about tasks
whose demands do not collide, and those are the ones that can genuinely run
together.

Two other things fit. With enough practice some tasks become automatic and
need little conscious thought, which frees them to run alongside something
else. And multi-object tracking, plausibly a useful skill for our ancestors,
looks optimized enough to hold several objects at once. Both are cases where
one task stays under the threshold.

Driving is where the switch is easy to feel. Unexpected road conditions take
the whole of attention, and everything else becomes something you come back
to.

## The missing term

Multitasking is a balance between what the brain can hold at once and what
the task costs it. The inputs really do arrive together and get handled
together, and the moment the demand outruns capacity the same system starts
taking turns. So asking whether multitasking is simultaneous attention or
rapid task switching leaves out a term. The load belongs in the description.
